# How to Play

Chess, but each side's king is a **ruler** with a special power. If you know chess, you already know most of the game. This guide covers what's different, and some tips for each ruler.

## What's different from normal chess

- **Capture the ruler to win.** There's no checkmate. You're allowed to leave your ruler in danger, but if the opponent captures it, you lose. When your ruler is attacked, its square turns red and a warning sound plays.
- **No safe moves left?** If every move you have puts your ruler in danger, you still have to pick one. Running out of safe moves (a stalemate in normal chess) is a loss.
- **Draws:** the same position three times is a draw. The turn light then parks in the middle in gray.
- **Pawns promote to a rook**, not a queen.
- **No en passant.** Pawns can only capture diagonally onto the square where the enemy piece stands.
- **Castling** works as usual. Your ruler can't castle out of, through, or into an attacked square.

## The bar beside the board

The bar to the right of the board is mirrored: the card at the top belongs to the player at the top, the card at the bottom to the player at the bottom.

- **Gray boxes** show each side's total material on the board (pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9).
- **Ruler boxes** show what each ruler's power has ready (for example, the Necromancer's grave).
- **The light** in the middle slides toward whoever's turn it is, and takes their color. When the game ends, it stays with the winner.

## The rulers

### King

No special power. A plain chess king, useful for learning the rules or for a normal game.

### Necromancer

Raises fallen enemies to fight for you.

**How it works**

- The square where the **most recent piece died** is the **grave** (marked with ☠). There's only one grave. Every new capture moves it.
- On your turn, instead of moving, you can **raise** the piece in the grave. It comes back on that square, on your side.
- You can only raise **enemy pieces**. If the most recent piece to die was yours, the grave is useless to you until the next capture.
- The grave square must be **empty**. Right after a capture, the capturing piece is standing on the grave, so it's blocked until that piece moves away.
- A raised pawn has already used its first move, so it can't move two squares.
- Your purple box in the side bar shows the piece you could raise. It's faded while the grave is blocked, and glows when you can raise it.
- When you can raise a piece, the grave glows purple. Click the grave or your purple box to raise.

**Tips for playing the Necromancer**

- **The threat is worth more than the piece.** If a strong enemy piece is in the grave, whatever stands on that square can't leave without handing it to you. It's effectively pinned there, which ties up the opponent.
- **Your own piece on the grave is a loaded trigger.** Move it away with a threat of its own (an attack or a check), and the opponent has two problems at once: deal with your threat, or let you raise the piece.
- **Watch your own trades.** When you lose a piece, it fills the grave and pushes out whatever enemy piece was in there. Every trade has a hidden cost.
- **Patience pays.** The Necromancer is better in slow games than in races. It gets stronger as the board empties: locking one of the opponent's last major pieces hurts a lot.

**Tips for playing against the Necromancer**

- **Replace the grave.** If the Necromancer just won one of your strong pieces, capture any of their pieces, even a pawn. Now the grave holds their piece, which they can't raise, and your strong piece is gone for good instead of coming back against you.
- **Keep the grave covered.** If a raised piece would just be captured again, raising it isn't a threat. Keep a piece aimed at the grave square and you can move freely.
- **Use the grave as bait.** While your queen is in the grave, a piece of yours that looks free might not be: if the Necromancer takes it, that piece replaces your queen in the grave.
- **Step off with care.** Moving your piece off the grave opens it. Do it when the raise wouldn't hurt, or when your move creates a bigger threat.

### Alchemist

Turns pieces into other pieces using a budget of points.

**How it works**

- On your turn, instead of moving, you can **convert** one of your pieces into another type. Your ruler can't be converted.
- Pieces are worth points: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9.
  - **Downgrading** earns the difference. A rook becoming a knight earns 2 points.
  - **Upgrading** costs the difference. A pawn becoming a bishop costs 2 points.
  - **Swapping** between equal pieces (knight and bishop) is free, but still uses your turn.
- Your budget is the **blue box** in the side bar. You usually start with 0, so your first conversion has to be a downgrade.
- **New pawns** can only be made in your own half of the board.
- A converted piece counts as having moved, so a new pawn can't move two squares.
- Select one of your pieces to see the **Convert to:** row under the board, with the price of each option. Greyed-out options aren't allowed right now; hover over one to see why.

**Tips for playing the Alchemist**

- **Fix your worst piece.** A rook stuck behind its pawns earns 2 points as an active knight. Downgrades are how you get going, and they can make your army better, not worse.
- **More pieces beat bigger pieces.** Two rooks that protect each other are often better than a queen, which can only harass. Don't assume an upgrade is always an improvement.
- **Switch shapes to fit the position.** A free knight-to-bishop swap can turn a blocked piece into an active one, or put a new attacker on a weak color.
- **A queen is a hunter.** Since capturing the ruler wins, a queen chasing an exposed ruler can end the game fast. Keep your ruler sheltered when the opponent has one.
- **A queen is a target.** Against a Necromancer, a freshly made queen is exactly the piece that hurts most when it lands in the enemy's grave.
